## Facts by topic

### The diver's equipment
- The nose clip, *al fatam* (also spelt fattam), kept water out of the nose and helped divers
  equalise pressure in the ears. Usually turtle shell or animal horn; the Qatar museum example is
  wood. A cord let it hang round the neck. The museum says it was first mentioned about a thousand
  years ago in early Arabic descriptions of pearl diving. (S1; S3 says bone)
- Divers sank with a stone or lead weight tied to the leg (*al thuql* or *al hijara*); a hauler on
  the boat pulled the weight back up. A second rope let the hauler pull the diver up when he ran out
  of breath. (S2, S3)
- Weights were dense stone, for example haematite (*lasif*); lead weights became common only towards
  the end of the industry in the early twentieth century. (S2) **Art note:** stone weights for most of Age V.
- *Al deyeen*: a rope or thread net basket worn round the neck for oysters. *Al khabt*: leather
  finger guards. *Al maflaqa*: a small curved knife to open oysters. *Al yada*: the rope linking the
  diver to the hauler (*al seeb*). Clothing: *al sirwal* shorts, *al shamshool* thick black fabric,
  a head cover against jellyfish. Wax or fat in the nostrils is also mentioned. (S3)

### The pearling economy and its end
- Pearling dominated the Gulf economy from about the second century until the early twentieth
  century, peaking at the end of the nineteenth and beginning of the twentieth. It suffered a sudden
  and catastrophic decline in the 1930s with the development of cultured pearls in Japan. (S4)
- The Smithsonian article puts the decline after Japanese cultured pearls reached the market in the
  1930s and estimates about 80% of the UAE's workers were involved in pearling in some way before the
  Second World War. (S6) **Treat the 80% figure as an estimate from one secondary source.**
- The GDD also names the world depression as a cause (GDD section 13); to confirm in Heard-Bey or Zahlan.

### Labour (flag H1, for the advisor; not used in game content yet)
- Hopper: enslaved Africans made up a third to a half of annual diving crews during the
  late-nineteenth-century boom; debt bound many divers. (S9)
- The Smithsonian article lists enslavement, abuse, shark attacks and hearing loss among divers'
  conditions, and says British agents offered manumission to enslaved divers who could prove abuse as
  the industry declined. (S6)

### Music on the pearling boats
- Fjiri (fijiri) dates back to the late nineteenth century and was performed by pearl divers and
  crews to express the hardships of the sea; it began on Muharraq. Performers sit in a circle with
  drums, finger chimes and a clay pot (*jahl*) played as an instrument; a lead singer conducts.
  Inscribed by UNESCO in 2021. (S5)
- The lead singer is the *nahham*, joined by a chorus singing, clapping and drumming. Rhythms blend
  Bedouin, East African, Persian and Indian styles, reflecting where crews came from. Songs often start
  slowly, build to a near-frantic peak and settle. Themes: love, God, memory, loss, joy. (S6)
- **Audio note:** voice, clapping and percussion lead. Melodic instruments (oud and others) belong to
  leisure and urban music; do not put an orchestra or oud on the boat itself.

### Houses on the coast
- The most common housing was the *arish*: palm-frond panels, woven tighter in winter and looser in
  summer, facing the prevailing wind; some had a wind tower. (S7)
- Coastal masonry used coral rock with gypsum-based mortar; inland, mud brick with mud mortar.
  Permanent houses were built round a courtyard, rarely more than one storey. (S7)
- *Barjeel* wind towers caught the wind to cool interiors. (S7) Dating varies by town: many surviving
  Dubai examples are early twentieth century. **Art note:** in a seventeenth to early twentieth-century
  town, wind towers should be few and belong to wealthy houses; most homes are arish.

### Ahmad ibn Majid and the kamal
- Born around 1432 in Julfar; known as the "Lion of the Sea". His best-known work is *Kitab
  al-fawa'id fi usul wa-l-qawa'id* (Book of Benefits in the Principles of Navigation), covering tides,
  monsoon winds, currents, distances between ports, latitude by the pole star and reefs. Some of his
  works are in verse. (S8, citing Agius, Clark, Lunde, Tibbetts)
- The kamal is a piece of wood on a knotted cord, used to measure latitude by sighting the pole
  star. (S8)
- **Contested:** whether Ibn Majid piloted Vasco da Gama to India. The source says historians
  disagree and he was probably not the pilot. The game must not claim he was. (S8)
- **Note:** Ibn Majid lived in the fifteenth century, before Age V. He joins the court as a leader
  from an earlier age, which the GDD allows (section 10). He has no invented dialogue.
